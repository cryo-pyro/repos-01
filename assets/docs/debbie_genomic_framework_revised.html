GST (Bertalanffy, 1968) — the structural upstream. Applied here to map why genomic sharing succeeds or fails before any ethical principle is invoked. It identifies interdependencies across individual, institutional, national, and international levels. In this study: GST frames the independent variables — age, gender, institutional policies, legal support, perceived risk/benefit. It shows that ethical possibility is bounded by systemic capacity. Without GST you cannot see what Principlism is working inside of.
Principlism (Beauchamp & Childress, 1979; emerged post-Tuskegee & Nuremberg) — the mediating constraint layer. Autonomy, Beneficence, Non-maleficence, Justice operate as hard limits on permissible action within the state space GST describes. Applied here to social/relational factors, community engagement practices, and ethical concerns around consent and re-identifiability. Principlism was itself a systemic response to systemic failures — a direct historical output of GST's feedback logic. In this study it mediates between structural capacity and just outcomes.
Distributive Justice (Rawls/Sen) — the falsifiable outcome. After GST maps the system and Principlism constrains the action space, DJ defines whether the result is just. Applied here to measure power asymmetries (LMIC vs. HIC), benefit sharing, and data sovereignty. It is the dependent variable's theoretical home: streamlined sharing is just sharing, not merely efficient sharing. The feedback arrow from inequitable outcomes back to GST closes the triangulation — if DJ finds failure, the error must be located upstream in the structural layer.
