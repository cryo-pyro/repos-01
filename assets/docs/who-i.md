### 🧭 Level 1 · Session 3 — **The Framework**
**Framing the Economic Question for AI**
*How do you structure a health financing or priority-setting question so AI can help you answer it?*

---

**Why this session exists**

When WHO health economists and financing specialists turn to AI tools for literature support, they often get back clinical trial summaries, drug efficacy data, or biomedical findings — none of which is what they came for. The reason is almost always the same: the question was framed in clinical language, so the AI searched clinical space.

The framework you use to pose your research question determines what the AI finds. This session introduces a question structure designed for health economics and financing work — and shows you how to translate it into the search strings and AI prompts that actually serve WHO India's mandate.

---

**From PICO to PECO-F**

The standard clinical framework is PICO: Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome. It works well for asking whether Drug A beats Drug B in a randomised trial. It does not work well for asking whether community health insurance schemes reduce catastrophic health expenditure among informal workers in low-income settings — which is closer to the kind of question Grace Kabaniha's team actually works on.

For health financing and priority-setting, the more useful frame is **PECO-F**:

| Element | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **P** — Population | Who bears the burden? | Informal sector households in Uttar Pradesh |
| **E** — Exposure / Intervention | What financing mechanism or policy? | Contributory health insurance vs. tax-funded coverage |
| **C** — Comparator | What is the alternative? | No coverage / out-of-pocket only |
| **O** — Outcome | What do we measure? | Catastrophic health expenditure, impoverishment, utilisation |
| **F** — Feasibility & Equity | What constraints apply? | Affordability, gender equity, willingness to pay |

The F element is not cosmetic. As Kabaniha's own PhD established, the *acceptability* and *equity* dimensions of an intervention can overturn a purely cost-effectiveness ranking — mass media HIV interventions, for instance, may score poorly on cost-effectiveness but rank highly on acceptability with local stakeholders. AI tools will not surface that tension unless you ask for it explicitly.

---

**Translating PECO-F into an AI prompt**

A poorly framed question:
> *"What is the best way to reduce out-of-pocket health spending in India?"*

This returns generic overviews, WHO reports from 2010, and surface-level policy summaries.

A well-framed question using PECO-F logic:
> *"I am looking for evidence on whether community-based health insurance schemes (E) reduce catastrophic health expenditure (O) among informal sector workers (P) in low- and middle-income countries (C: compared to no insurance), with attention to equity outcomes and willingness-to-pay thresholds (F). Please prioritise systematic reviews and economic evaluations published after 2015."*

The second prompt gives the AI a scoped retrieval task. It also signals the methodological register — systematic reviews, economic evaluations — so the tool does not return qualitative ethnographies or clinical guidelines.

---

**The priority-setting variant**

When the question is about *allocating scarce resources across interventions* — Grace Kabaniha's PhD domain — the frame shifts slightly. Here the question is not just "does this intervention work?" but "should this be funded, given competing claims on the same budget?"

For these questions, add two more dimensions:

- **Budget envelope**: What resource constraint are we working within?
- **Decision rule**: Are we optimising for cost per DALY averted? For equity-weighted coverage? For political acceptability?

Example:
> *"What frameworks have been used to compare the cost-effectiveness and equity impact of expanding community health insurance versus increasing public facility capacity for primary care in LMICs? I need approaches that go beyond cost-per-DALY to include equity weighting and stakeholder acceptability — relevant to a national UHC reform context."*

That is a question AI can navigate. It invokes the ACE (Assessing Cost-Effectiveness) literature, the A4R (Accountability for Reasonableness) framework, and the health financing reform literature simultaneously — which is exactly where WHO India's work sits.

---

**Practical exercise**

---

**Key takeaway**

The framework is not bureaucracy — it is precision. A structured question reduces the chance that AI returns the wrong kind of evidence. For WHO staff working on UHC, financing, and priority-setting, that means explicitly naming populations, mechanisms, comparators, outcomes, and equity constraints every time. The tools are only as good as the question you bring.

            <h2>Why this session exists</h2>
            <p>When WHO health economists and financing specialists turn to AI tools for literature support, they often get back clinical trial summaries, drug efficacy data, or biomedical findings — none of which is what they came for. The reason is almost always the same: the question was framed in clinical language, so the AI searched clinical space.</p>
            <p>The framework you use to pose your research question determines what the AI finds. This session introduces a question structure designed for health economics and financing work — and shows you how to translate it into the search strings and AI prompts that actually serve WHO India's mandate.</p>

            <h2>From PICO to PECO-F</h2>
            <p>The standard clinical framework is PICO: Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome. It works well for asking whether Drug A beats Drug B in a randomised trial. It does not work well for asking whether community health insurance schemes reduce catastrophic health expenditure among informal workers in low-income settings — which is closer to the kind of question Grace Kabaniha’s team actually works on.</p>
            <p>For health financing and priority-setting, the more useful frame is <strong>PECO-F</strong>:</p>

            <table>
                <tr><th>Element</th><th>What it means</th><th>Example</th></tr>
                <tr><td><strong>P</strong> — Population</td><td>Who bears the burden?</td><td>Informal sector households in Uttar Pradesh</td></tr>
                <tr><td><strong>E</strong> — Exposure / Intervention</td><td>What financing mechanism or policy?</td><td>Contributory health insurance vs. tax-funded coverage</td></tr>
                <tr><td><strong>C</strong> — Comparator</td><td>What is the alternative?</td><td>No coverage / out-of-pocket only</td></tr>
                <tr><td><strong>O</strong> — Outcome</td><td>What do we measure?</td><td>Catastrophic health expenditure, impoverishment, utilisation</td></tr>
                <tr><td><strong>F</strong> — Feasibility & Equity</td><td>What constraints apply?</td><td>Affordability, gender equity, willingness to pay</td></tr>
            </table>

            <p>The <em>F</em> element is not cosmetic. As Kabaniha’s own PhD established, the <em>acceptability</em> and <em>equity</em> dimensions of an intervention can overturn a purely cost-effectiveness ranking. AI tools will not surface that tension unless you ask for it explicitly.</p>

            <h2>Translating PECO-F into an AI prompt</h2>
            <p><strong>A poorly framed question:</strong></p>
            <blockquote>“What is the best way to reduce out-of-pocket health spending in India?”</blockquote>
            <p>This returns generic overviews and old WHO reports.</p>

            <p><strong>A well-framed question using PECO-F logic:</strong></p>
            <blockquote>“I am looking for evidence on whether community-based health insurance schemes (E) reduce catastrophic health expenditure (O) among informal sector workers (P) in low- and middle-income countries (C: compared to no insurance), with attention to equity outcomes and willingness-to-pay thresholds (F). Please prioritise systematic reviews and economic evaluations published after 2015.”</blockquote>

            <h2>The priority-setting variant (Grace Kabaniha’s domain)</h2>
            <p>When the question is about allocating scarce resources, add two more dimensions:</p>
            <ul>
                <li><strong>Budget envelope</strong>: What resource constraint are we working within?</li>
                <li><strong>Decision rule</strong>: Are we optimising for cost per DALY averted? For equity-weighted coverage? For political acceptability?</li>
            </ul>
            <p><strong>Example prompt:</strong></p>
            <blockquote>“What frameworks have been used to compare the cost-effectiveness and equity impact of expanding community health insurance versus increasing public facility capacity for primary care in LMICs? I need approaches that go beyond cost-per-DALY to include equity weighting and stakeholder acceptability — relevant to a national UHC reform context.”</blockquote>

            <div class="exercise-box">
                <h3>🧪 Practical exercise (do this now)</h3>
                <p>Take one live research question your team is currently working on. Write it out in plain language, then reframe it using PECO-F. Then convert that into an AI prompt. In the next session you will use that prompt with actual tools and compare what you get.</p>
            </div>

            <h2>Key takeaway</h2>
            <p>The framework is not bureaucracy — it is precision. A structured question reduces the chance that AI returns the wrong kind of evidence. For WHO staff working on UHC, financing, and priority-setting, that means explicitly naming populations, mechanisms, comparators, outcomes, and equity constraints every time. The tools are only as good as the question you bring.</p>
