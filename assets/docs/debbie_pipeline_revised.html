General Systems Theory — the full picture before simplification
GST (Bertalanffy, 1968) argues that no single variable can be understood in isolation from the system that produced it. Applied here: before any ethical principle is invoked, the study maps all factors together — individual, institutional, and perceptual — as an interconnected whole. This is why the framework begins with structure, not ethics.
Independent variables — the dimensions of the system:

Individual: age, gender
Institutional: policies, norms, legal support, regulator oversight, REC members
Perceptual: perceived risk, perceived benefit

Principlism — the ethical filter applied to the system
Once the system is mapped, Principlism (Beauchamp & Childress, 1979) acts as the framework's ethical constraint layer. It applies four weights — autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice — to the variables identified by GST. Where those weights are insufficient (weak legal support, high perceived risk, absent community engagement), the pathway to sharing is blocked.
Why Principlism follows GST — the historical relay:

The Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972) and Nazi medical experiments demonstrated that without systemic oversight, ethical principles collapse entirely. The international response — the Nuremberg Code (1947), the Belmont Report (1979), and Beauchamp & Childress — was itself a systemic correction. Principlism emerged from the failure of systems, which is precisely why it sits downstream of GST in this framework. The arrow is not theoretical — it is historical.
Vector — the direction produced by the ethical filter
Applying Principlism to the system does not immediately produce an outcome. It produces a direction of action — the actual conduct of genomic data sharing as operationalised in this study's methods. The quality of this direction depends on the upstream conditions: strong systems and sound ethics produce streamlined sharing; weak systems and ethical gaps produce friction or refusal.
Community engagement runs continuously across three phases:

Before: community mapping, sensitisation, consent design
During: informed consent, ongoing dialogue, participant autonomy
After: return of findings, benefit sharing, re-consent where needed
Invariant (eigen___) — what remains true across all conditions
As the study's methods operate under the framework's ethical constraints, certain patterns emerge that hold regardless of which specific variables are in play. These are the core findings of the thesis. The blank is intentional — the invariant cannot be named before the data is collected. It might be trust, it might be sovereignty, it might be the irreducibility of community engagement. The framework creates the conditions for identifying it; it does not presuppose it.
This is how the framework influences interpretation: a finding is only treated as meaningful if it survives the ethical filter. A pattern that appears in the data but disappears when autonomy or justice constraints are applied is noise, not signal.
Distributive Justice — the earned outcome, not the assumed one
The final outcome — equitable, streamlined genomic data sharing — is reached only when the full pipeline has run: the system has been mapped (GST), the ethical weights have been applied (Principlism), and the stable patterns have been identified. Justice is not a starting assumption. It is the result of a process.
Feedback loop: if the outcome is inequitable, the error is upstream — in the system's structure (GST layer), not in the ethics. This is what makes the framework useful for interpretation: it locates the source of failure rather than simply noting that failure occurred.
