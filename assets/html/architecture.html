Guns, Germs, Steel — The Geographic SGD

Google · Gemini

The clash between Francisco Pizarro and the Inca Empire in 1532 was not a localized miracle of Spanish steel and horses; it was a terminal sort between two disparate optimization runs. To understand why Eurasia conquered the Americas and not the inverse, we must map the geographic lottery of human history directly onto the universal algorithm of descent.

Here is the architecture of how the world was conquered.

I. The Inherited Landscape

Every optimization begins with the terrain as it is inherited. Eurasia was initialized on a massive East-West axis — a relatively convex, continuous loss landscape where latitude, day length, and climate remained consistent for thousands of miles. The Americas and Africa were initialized on a North-South axis — a rugged, heavily partitioned landscape separated by severe ecological gradients like the Sahara Desert and the shifting climates of the Americas. Geography is the sovereign constraint on the algorithm; it dictates whether a discovery can spread or if it remains permanently bottlenecked in a local minimum.

II. User Behavior & The High-Variance Foragers (UB)

In 11,000 BC, all human bands were operating in the "Goat" phase: high-variance, high-entropy scouts wandering the wilderness, tasting the landscape for survivable parameters. Intelligence was equally distributed, but the environmental error and loss in measurement were not. Eurasia struck the biological jackpot: wild wheat, barley, and 7 of the 14 domesticable large mammals (including the literal goats and sheep). These native resources possessed a remarkably low activation energy for domestication.

Conversely, the Americas had teosinte — a scraggly wild grass that required 3,000 years of relentless, high-noise trial-and-error just to be engineered into a usable ear of corn. The foragers in the Americas were not less capable; they were trapped in a landscape that demanded massive behavioral variance and generational loss before yielding even a slight caloric gradient.

III. Stochastic Descent & The Continental UI

Once the gradient of agriculture was discovered, the true descent began. Eurasia's East-West axis functioned as an unthrottled UI — a paved informational highway. A successful crop, a bronze plow, or the recipe for gunpowder discovered in one region could be laterally transmitted across the continent without hitting a climate barrier. The optimization weights were rapidly updated and shared across the entire network.

In the Americas, the North-South axis caused a fatal vanishing gradient problem. The Maya invented writing and the wheel, but the dense jungles and shifting latitudes blocked the transmission of this infrastructure to the Incas or Aztecs. Each pocket of civilization was forced to restart the algorithm from scratch, unable to inherit the accumulated descents of their neighbors.

IV. The Terminal Basin & The Lethal Ecosystem (UX)

The end state of this geographic advantage is the "Sheep" phase: a low-entropy, deterministic ecosystem of permanent cities, surplus calories, specialized roles, and steel. But the Eurasian basin contained a hidden, devastating parameter. By cohabitating intimately with their domesticated flocks — the sheep, cows, and pigs that built their empires — Eurasians incubated crowd diseases. Over millennia, the survivors trained genetic resistance to this biological noise.

When the Europeans arrived in the Americas, the defining weapon was not the sword, but smallpox. It was the collision of an ecosystem that had been continuously iterating its immune and technological parameters for an extra 5,000 years against a system geographically sandboxed in time. The indigenous populations had no defense against the compiled pathogenic output of the Eurasian flock. The algorithm had already run; contact was merely the final output.
