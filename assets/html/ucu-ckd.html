The Scale of the Problem

How Big Is Uganda's Kidney Crisis?

Kidney disease is largely silent — patients often have no symptoms until more than half their kidney function is already lost. By the time they reach a clinic, options are few, expensive, and geographically concentrated in Kampala. The numbers below give a sense of the problem's scale.

2–7%
General population with kidney disease
Between 2 and 7 in every hundred Ugandans have some form of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Among people living with HIV — roughly 1.4 million Ugandans — the rate rises to about 15 in every hundred. Most have no idea.
PMC9438417 · Global Dialysis Perspective: Uganda
12.5%
CKD prevalence in eastern Uganda
Eastern Uganda carries more than three times the kidney disease burden of southwestern Uganda, where the rate is 3.9%. A difference this large — within a single country — cannot be explained away. It demands a district-level investigation to understand why, and to direct resources accordingly.
Muiru et al. · SEARCH-CKD study · PLOS One 2020
123+
Districts with no published kidney data
Of Uganda's 135 administrative districts, fewer than twelve have any published kidney disease data. The remaining 123 — home to tens of millions of people — are a complete blank on the scientific map. This is the gap we are closing.
Ukubona Atlas Landscape Survey · 2026
4
Dialysis centres in the whole country
Uganda's 44 million people are served by four dialysis centres, concentrated in Kampala and regional towns. Mulago and Kiruddu hospitals together provide approximately 2,600 dialysis sessions per month — a fraction of what is needed. A patient in Kaabong or Kotido district faces a journey of hundreds of kilometres for each treatment.
Daily Monitor Uganda · 2024
≈ 0
Kidney transplants performed in Uganda*
Uganda's first domestic kidney transplants were performed at Mulago Hospital in 2023 — a historic milestone. Fewer than ten procedures were completed before the programme was suspended in 2024 pending a regulatory framework. Every patient who needs a transplant is currently referred abroad, to Kenya, India or Turkey.
*Mulago pilot suspended 2024 · Monitor Uganda
$30,000
Cost of a kidney transplant abroad
A kidney transplant in India — the most common destination for Ugandan patients — costs between $25,000 and $35,000 US dollars, out of pocket. Estimates suggest a domestic programme, once established, could deliver the same procedure for around $8,000. The economic case for domestic investment is overwhelming.
Africanews · Uganda Parliament debate · 2022

What Drives Kidney Disease in Uganda

Risk Factors: A Profile
Unlike the West

The causes of kidney disease in Uganda are not identical to those in high-income countries. HIV infection is a major driver. Hypertension — affecting roughly one in four Ugandan adults — is the single largest contributor. But the most striking finding is how many cases remain unexplained by any known risk factor.

The single most important finding from the SEARCH-CKD study — the largest multi-country kidney disease survey in rural East Africa — is that two thirds of CKD cases in the region have no identifiable risk factor from the standard clinical checklist. This is not a data quality problem. It strongly suggests that Uganda-specific factors — endemic infections, traditional herbal medicines that are toxic to the kidneys, agricultural chemicals, or genetic variants common in eastern African populations — are driving a substantial share of the disease burden. These factors are entirely unstudied.

This is precisely why a district-level atlas is the most important scientific investment Uganda can make in kidney health: before anything can be prevented or treated, the burden must first be measured, and its distribution understood.

The Transplant Crisis

Sixty Years with No
Kidney Transplant Programme

Uganda is one of very few countries in sub-Saharan Africa that has never established a sustained kidney transplant programme. For sixty years, patients wealthy enough to travel have gone to India, Kenya, or Turkey. Those who cannot afford it have faced dialysis — if they are lucky enough to live near a centre — or death. The timeline below traces this history and charts where we are headed.

1960s – 1990s
Uganda gains independence but inherits almost no kidney care infrastructure. Patients with end-stage kidney failure receive palliative care only. Kenya begins building transplant capacity at Kenyatta National Hospital. Ugandan patients begin making the journey to Nairobi.
2000s – 2010s
India — particularly Bangalore and Mumbai — emerges as the primary transplant destination for Ugandan patients. Cost: $25,000–$35,000 per procedure, entirely out-of-pocket. As of 2012, Uganda has just two dialysis chairs and two specialist nephrologists in the entire country. Informal transplant tourism routes are well-established.
2020
Uganda's Parliament passes the Human Organ Donation and Tissue Transplant Bill. The law — still awaiting Presidential assent — would designate licensed transplant centres, regulate donor consent, and permit domestic transplants for the first time. Significant progress, but implementation remains stalled.
2022
Uganda's dialysis capacity has grown substantially: 94 chairs and 14 nephrologists nationally — remarkable growth from 2012. Parliament debates cost estimates: a domestic transplant would cost roughly $8,000 versus $30,000 abroad. The economic and equity argument for a domestic programme is clear.
December 2023 – 2024
Mulago National Referral Hospital performs Uganda's first kidney transplants — a historic milestone. A small team of Ugandan surgeons, with support from a visiting Indian surgical team, completes approximately four to ten procedures. The programme is suspended in 2024 while the Ministry of Health develops a regulatory framework. Patients return to the medical tourism queue.
March 2026 — Now
This collaboration begins. Ukubona LLC and Uganda Christian University launch the first systematic, district-level mapping of kidney disease burden across Uganda. The atlas will generate the preliminary data required for NIH NIDDK and Wellcome Trust grant applications, and provide the scientific underpinning for Uganda's domestic transplant programme reinstatement.
Month 4 → Beyond
First grant applications submitted based on atlas preliminary data. UCU-led student publications from Phase II. Expansion to mental health, HIV, and maternal health disease layers. UCU becomes the national reference centre for district-level disease surveillance in Uganda.

How This Project Works

A Living Map, Not
a One-Off Survey

Most disease mapping projects produce a single report that becomes outdated within a few years. This project is designed differently: the atlas is a living platform that improves with each research cycle, each new dataset, and each student cohort that contributes to it. Every new piece of evidence moves the map closer to reality.

The core logic is straightforward. We begin with what the published literature already tells us — patchy, concentrated in a handful of districts, but sufficient to establish a starting point. We identify the places where the evidence is weakest: those are the districts most urgently in need of study. We then deploy UCU students and public health researchers to close those gaps systematically, district by district, working outward from where data is richest toward where it is entirely absent.

The atlas is the output of each cycle — a publicly accessible, interactively browsable map that any clinician, health planner, policymaker, or researcher can consult to understand the kidney disease landscape across Uganda. It will be hosted at UCU and linked from the National Planning Authority's health data portal.

The platform is also designed to expand. Once the CKD and kidney disease layers are established — our first case study — the same infrastructure can be used to map mental health, HIV, maternal mortality, hypertension, and diabetes burden at district level. This makes it directly relevant to the NPA's stated priority of mental health surveillance, and positions UCU as Uganda's leading institution for public health intelligence.

Funding Strategy

Realistic Paths to
Sustained Funding

The combination of Dr. Muzaale's NIH track record, Hopkins institutional credibility, UCU's community access, and Prof. Basaza's policy network opens funding channels that would be inaccessible to either institution working alone. Below are the four most realistic pathways.

NIH National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases — R21 Exploratory Grant
$275,000 · 2 years
Fit: Very strong. Dr. Muzaale's K08-AG065520 — a prestigious NIH career development award on kidney donor outcomes — is a direct methodological predecessor to this work. An R21 application for "characterising chronic kidney disease epidemiology in sub-Saharan Africa using geospatial mapping methods" aligns with the NIDDK's published global health priorities (PA-21-213). UCU provides community access; Hopkins provides methods rigour and grant management infrastructure. The preliminary data produced in Phase I–II is precisely what this application needs.
Wellcome Trust — African Programmes in Science
£150,000 – £500,000 · 3–5 years
Fit: Strong. Wellcome's Science in Africa initiative specifically supports early-career African scientists embedded in African institutions generating high-quality research with direct policy impact. Prof. Basaza and the UCU student pipeline make this a compelling application. The CKD–HIV intersection — particularly the high burden of kidney disease among people living with HIV in Uganda — aligns with Wellcome's priority area at the boundary of infectious and non-communicable disease.
Uganda National Planning Authority — Health Intelligence Pilot
$30,000 – $80,000 · Proof of concept
Fit: Moderate, with strategy. The NPA's stated 2026 priority is mental health surveillance. Our approach is to present kidney disease as the proof-of-concept for a platform that will deliver mental health data in Phase II. The hook: depression is twice as prevalent in patients with CKD, meaning a kidney surveillance platform is simultaneously a mental health data infrastructure. The NPA gets a demonstration of something it cannot currently build internally.
International Society of Nephrology — SOROCA Programme
$10,000 – $50,000 · 1 year
Fit: Very strong for seed funding. ISN's Special Operations for Renal Outcomes in Central Africa grant is designed exactly for this type of low-resource CKD mapping project. The UCU–Hopkins partnership is the ideal profile: local African institutional capacity combined with international methodological credibility. SOROCA funding can cover Phase I–II costs and generate the preliminary data for the larger NIH R21 application that follows.
