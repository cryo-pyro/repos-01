The Scale of the Crisis

How Big Is Uganda's Kidney Crisis?

Kidney disease is largely silent — patients often have no symptoms until more than half their kidney function is already lost. By the time they reach a clinic, options are few, expensive, and geographically concentrated in Kampala. The numbers below give a sense of the scale. The case study that follows gives it a face.

2–7%
General population with kidney disease
Between 2 and 7 in every hundred Ugandans have some form of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Among people living with HIV — roughly 1.4 million Ugandans — the rate rises to approximately 15 in every hundred. Most have no idea.
PMC9438417 · Global Dialysis Perspective: Uganda
12.5%
CKD prevalence in eastern Uganda
Eastern Uganda carries more than three times the kidney disease burden of southwestern Uganda, where the rate is 3.9%. A difference this large — within a single country — cannot be explained away. It demands a district-level investigation and a 146-district atlas to understand why.
Muiru et al. · SEARCH-CKD study · PLOS One 2020
134+
Districts with no published kidney data
Of Uganda's 146 administrative districts (2026 Electoral Commission), fewer than twelve have any published kidney disease data. The remaining 134 — home to tens of millions of people — are a complete blank on the scientific map. This is the gap we are closing.
Ukubona Atlas Landscape Survey · 2026 · Updated per Basaza correction
4
Dialysis centres in the whole country
Uganda's 46 million people are served by four dialysis centres, concentrated in Kampala and regional towns. A patient in Kaabong or Kotido district faces a journey of hundreds of kilometres for each treatment session.
Daily Monitor Uganda · 2024
≈ 0
Domestic kidney transplants currently active
Uganda's first domestic kidney transplants were performed at Mulago Hospital in 2023 — fewer than ten procedures before the programme was suspended in 2024 pending a regulatory framework. Every patient who needs a transplant is currently referred abroad, to Kenya, India, or Turkey.
Mulago pilot suspended 2024 · Monitor Uganda
$30–55K
Cost of a kidney transplant abroad
A kidney transplant in India — the most common destination — costs $25,000–$35,000 out of pocket. Emergency cases can reach $55,000. Estimates suggest a domestic programme could deliver the same procedure for approximately $8,000. The economic case for investment is overwhelming.
Africanews · Uganda Parliament debate 2022 · Live case, March 2026

"In Uganda, about two percent of the population have end-stage kidney disease, and many are aware that non-communicable diseases are on the rise — twenty-five percent of the population are hypertensive."

— Uganda Ministry of Health official · Mulago kidney programme briefing · 2024

What Drives Kidney Disease in Uganda

Risk Factors: A Profile
Unlike the West

The causes of kidney disease in Uganda are not identical to those in high-income countries. HIV infection is a major driver. Hypertension — affecting roughly one in four Ugandan adults — is the single largest contributor. But the most striking finding from the SEARCH-CKD study is how many cases remain entirely unexplained by any known risk factor. That unknown column alone justifies a 146-district investigation.

The single most important finding from the SEARCH-CKD study is that two thirds of CKD cases in rural East Africa have no identifiable risk factor from the standard clinical checklist. This is not a data quality problem. It strongly suggests Uganda-specific drivers — endemic infections, nephrotoxic traditional herbal medicines, agricultural chemicals, or genetic variants common in East African populations — are responsible for a substantial share of the burden. These hypotheses are entirely unstudied.

This is precisely why a district-level atlas is the most important scientific investment Uganda can make in kidney health: before anything can be prevented or treated, the burden must first be measured and its distribution understood.

Today's transplant pathway — for every Ugandan patient
Step 1
Patient presents with end-stage renal disease at Mulago or Kiruddu. Dialysis begins — if a slot is available. Many patients present too late for any intervention.
Step 2
Family launches a fundraising campaign. Target: $30,000–$55,000 USD. Most campaigns do not reach target. Those that do face months of uncertainty.
Step 3
If funds raised: travel to India, Turkey, or Kenya. Three to six months abroad for surgery and recovery. Family must accompany. Livelihoods disrupted.
Step 4
Returns to Uganda. No domestic infrastructure for immunosuppression monitoring or long-term follow-up. Outcomes poorly tracked. The system loses the patient again.
Atlas
The Atlas makes Step 0 visible: where are cases forming, in which districts, at what stage, and why — before they ever reach Mulago. That is the intervention point.

The Transplant Crisis

Sixty Years with No
Kidney Transplant Programme

Uganda is one of very few countries in sub-Saharan Africa that has never established a sustained kidney transplant programme. For six decades, patients wealthy enough to travel have gone to India, Kenya, or Turkey. Those who could not afford it have faced dialysis — if they are close enough to a centre — or death. The timeline below traces this history and charts where we are headed.

1960s – 1990s
Uganda gains independence but inherits almost no kidney care infrastructure. Patients with end-stage kidney failure receive palliative care only. Kenya begins building transplant capacity at Kenyatta National Hospital. Ugandan patients begin making the journey to Nairobi.
2000s – 2010s
India — particularly Bangalore and Mumbai — emerges as the primary transplant destination. Cost: $25,000–$35,000 out of pocket. As of 2012, Uganda had just two dialysis chairs and two specialist nephrologists in the entire country. Informal transplant tourism routes become well-established.
2020
Uganda's Parliament passes the Human Organ Donation and Tissue Transplant Bill — designating licensed transplant centres, regulating donor consent, and permitting domestic transplants for the first time. Significant progress, but implementation remains stalled pending Presidential assent.
2022
Uganda's dialysis capacity has grown substantially: 94 chairs and 14 nephrologists nationally — remarkable growth from 2012. Parliament debates cost estimates: a domestic transplant would cost roughly $8,000 versus $30,000 abroad. The economic and equity argument for domestic capacity is now on the parliamentary record.
December 2023 – 2024
Mulago National Referral Hospital performs Uganda's first kidney transplants — a historic milestone. A small team of Ugandan surgeons, with support from a visiting Indian surgical team, completes approximately four to ten procedures. The programme is then suspended while the Ministry of Health develops a regulatory framework. Patients return to the medical tourism queue.

How This Project Works

A Living Map, Not
a One-Off Survey

Most disease mapping projects produce a single report that becomes outdated within a few years. This project is designed differently: the atlas is a living platform that improves with each research cycle, each new dataset, and each student cohort that contributes to it.

The core logic is straightforward. We begin with what the published literature already tells us — patchy, concentrated in a handful of districts, but sufficient to establish a starting point. We identify the places where the evidence is weakest: those are the districts most urgently in need of study. UCU students and public health researchers then close those gaps systematically, district by district, working outward from where data is richest toward where it is entirely absent.

The atlas is the output of each cycle — a publicly accessible, interactively browsable map that any clinician, health planner, policymaker, or researcher can consult. It will be hosted at UCU and linked from the National Planning Authority's health data portal.

The platform is designed to expand. Once the CKD layers are established, the same infrastructure maps mental health, HIV, maternal mortality, hypertension, and diabetes at district level — directly relevant to the NPA's 2026 priority of mental health surveillance, and positioning UCU as Uganda's leading institution for public health intelligence.
