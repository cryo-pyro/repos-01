Mis-Specified Consent · Formal Essay

Abstract: Informed consent is often treated as a procedural requirement in medicine. Yet in practice, it frequently fails — not due to lack of data, but due to a deeper structural issue: the physician and patient are solving fundamentally different optimization problems. Clinical decision-making should be reframed as a problem of loss function alignment, where outcomes, probabilities, and — critically — patient-specific weights are made explicit. Without this, consent becomes nominal rather than real.

Modern clinical practice assumes that if risks and benefits are disclosed, consent is achieved. But disclosure alone does not guarantee understanding, nor does it ensure alignment between physician intent and patient values. Thus, what is labeled "consent" is often decision-making under semantic ambiguity.

The failure of informed consent in modern medicine is not primarily informational — it is structural. It arises from misaligned models of reality. The solution is not more disclosure, but explicit co-construction of the loss function that governs the decision itself.

A more rigorous consent model requires three components: 1. STRUCTURED ALTERNATIVES Surgical / Conservative (defined in patient's terms) / No intervention 2. QUANTIFIED OUTCOMES Probabilities · Time horizons · Functional consequences Not vague risks — lived realities 3. EXPLICIT WEIGHTING What matters most: longevity, mobility, independence, comfort? What outcomes are unacceptable, regardless of probability? Without step 3, steps 1 and 2 are insufficient.

Risk calculators (e.g., for mortality, ESRD, hospitalization) are increasingly used in clinical care. However, they typically output probabilities without incorporating patient-specific weights. More precise numbers do not produce better decisions if the objective function remains undefined. A calculator without personalization of weights just produces better confusion.
