Overview

The Central Paradox

Every efficient run of gradient descent in deep learning operates with a stepsize large enough to cause instability. This is not a bug — it is the mechanism. The tutorial presents a rigorous theory of why training instability arises, why it is inseparable from efficiency, and how large stepsizes simultaneously accelerate optimization and prevent overfitting.

Part 1: Optimization

Theory and insights through clean examples. Large stepsizes cause non-monotone loss but achieve faster convergence than stable small-stepsize runs. The acceleration is provable, and in key settings matches or beats Nesterov momentum.

Part 2: Generalization

Large stepsizes impose a sharpness penalty on the minimizer found, preferring flatter minima that generalize better. Instability during training is not noise — it is the selection mechanism for good solutions.

Motivation

Why Loss Spikes

The gradient descent update $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \cdot \nabla L(\theta_t)$ can fail for four distinct reasons. They are not equally fundamental — and only one of them is inherent.

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \underbrace{\eta}_{\text{stepsize}} \cdot \underbrace{\nabla L(\theta_t)}_{\text{"gradient"}}$$
Data randomness
Unlucky mini-batch draws. Manageable — larger batch size reduces variance.
Numerical overflow
Insufficient floating-point precision. Manageable — use higher precision or gradient clipping.
Loss landscape
Varying layer-wise curvature. Partially manageable — architecture choices matter.
Inherent instability
Stepsize / learning rate. In deep learning, all efficient stepsizes are "large," causing training instability. This is unavoidable if you want to train efficiently.
Key insight from the sandboxes

Even with no randomness, mild overflow, and a convex landscape (logistic regression on MNIST), efficient runs of GD are still unstable. Instability is not a pathology of deep learning specifically — it is an inherent property of large-stepsize gradient descent.

Framework

The Stepsize Spectrum

The relationship between stepsize $\eta$ and convergence behavior defines three qualitatively distinct regimes. Classical theory covers only the leftmost. Practice lives in the middle. The rightmost is always divergent.

Stable · monotone ↓
Unstable → convergent
practice lives here
Divergent ✗
η → 0 η < 2/λ η ≈ 2/λ (edge of stability) η → ∞

Gradient flow ($\eta \to 0$): always stable, always converges, never efficient.

Edge of stability ($\eta \approx 2/\lambda_{max}$): sharpness oscillates around threshold; loss is non-monotone but training succeeds. Cohen et al. (ICLR 2021) showed this is the typical regime for neural network training.

Large stepsize ($\eta \gg 2/\lambda_{max}$): does not exist for quadratics (always diverges), but can converge for non-quadratic losses like logistic regression. This is the key insight enabling the tutorial's acceleration results.

$$\text{Descent Lemma: } L(\theta_{t+1}) \leq L(\theta_t) \iff \eta < \frac{2}{\sup\|\nabla^2 L(\cdot)\|}$$

The descent lemma is the cornerstone of classical optimization theory. It is also what the practice of deep learning systematically violates. The tutorial's contribution is showing that this violation, when controlled, is beneficial.

Part 1 — Optimization

Large Stepsizes Accelerate

The main positive result for optimization: large stepsizes create an unstable phase followed by a stable phase. The instability is transient and productive — the loss descends on average even when it oscillates, and the stable phase converges at rate $O(1/\eta t)$, faster by a factor of $\eta$ than the classical $O(1/t)$.

The Three Effects of Large Stepsize (Logistic Regression)

For GD on separable logistic regression with any stepsize $\eta$:

$$\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1} L(\theta_k)^2 = \tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{1+\eta^2}{\eta t}\right)$$

1. Asymptotic rate $1/(\eta t)$ → doubling $\eta$ doubles speed.
2. Phase transition in $\tau = \Theta(\eta)$ steps → longer unstable phase for larger $\eta$.
3. If $T \geq \Theta(n)$ and $\eta = \Theta(T)$, then $L(\theta_T) = \tilde{O}(1/T^2)$ — matching Nesterov acceleration.

The lower bound

If $\eta$ is such that $L(\theta_t) \downarrow$ for all $t$ (monotone descent, classical regime), then $L(\theta_t) = \Omega(1/t)$. Monotone training cannot achieve $O(1/T^2)$. The instability is not just tolerable — it is necessary for the acceleration.

Adaptive Stepsize: Even Faster

The adaptive variant modifies the update to $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \cdot \frac{\nabla L(\theta_t)}{L(\theta_t)}$, adapting to the local curvature of the logistic loss. Ji & Telgarsky (ALT 2021) showed this achieves $L(\theta_t) \leq \exp(-\Theta(t))$ for $\eta = \Theta(1)$ — exponential convergence with a constant stepsize.

For separable data with margin $\gamma$, the large adaptive stepsize achieves:

$$L(\bar\theta_t) \leq \exp\!\left(-\Theta(\gamma^2 \eta t)\right)$$

For $\eta \to \infty$: arbitrarily small error in $1/\gamma^2$ steps. This matches the Perceptron lower bound (Novikoff, 1962) — the adaptive large stepsize is minimax optimal.

The valley + basin picture

In the ℓ₂-regularized case, training has two phases geometrically: an unstable "valley" phase where the parameter overshoots far from the minimizer (‖θ‖ = O(poly(κ))), followed by a stable "basin" phase where it contracts back to ‖θ̃‖ = O(ln κ). The large stepsize enables fast traversal of the valley that classical small-stepsize GD cannot achieve.

Part 2 — Generalization

Large Stepsizes Prefer Flat Minima

The second major theme: training instability is not merely tolerated — it is the mechanism by which SGD selects for generalizable solutions.

The Sharpness Penalty

If label-noise SGD converges, under suitable assumptions, the minimizer $\theta_\infty$ satisfies a sharpness penalty:

$$\text{tr}(\nabla^2 L(\theta_\infty)) < O(1/\eta)$$

Larger stepsize → smaller allowed sharpness → flatter minimizer. The tradeoff is explicit:

Large η cost

Training instability: $L(\theta_t)$ oscillates for $t = 1, 2, \ldots$

Large η benefit

Minimizer flatness: $|L(\theta_\infty + \epsilon) - L(\theta_\infty)|$ is small → better generalization.

Progressive sharpening & edge of stability

Even starting from a point satisfying the descent lemma, sharpness $\|\nabla^2 L\|$ increases along the GD path until it hits $2/\eta$ (edge of stability), after which it oscillates around this threshold. This progressive sharpening means every efficient run is pushed to the edge — and held there — by the dynamics of gradient descent itself.

Dynamical Stability Perspective

Wu, Ma & E (NeurIPS 2018) showed that if GD with large $\eta$ converges to a stationary point, then in regular cases:

$$\|\nabla^2 L(\theta_\infty)\| < \frac{2}{\eta} \quad \text{(sharpness penalty)}$$

This is why SGD selects flat minima: sharp minima are dynamically unstable under large-stepsize GD and are therefore never the attractors. The implicit bias toward flat minima is a direct consequence of the training instability.

Conceptual Framework

The Alternative Mental Model

The standard mental model pairs theory (gradient flow, small η, always stable) with practice (deep learning, large η, sometimes unstable). The gap is treated as something to be apologized for. The tutorial proposes a different framing:

Logistic regression is the Rosetta Stone: complex enough to exhibit the non-quadratic behavior responsible for large-stepsize convergence, simple enough to prove theorems about. The tutorial uses it as the bridge between classical optimization theory (where instability is impossible for convex quadratics) and the observed behavior of deep networks.

Open Problems

Eight Open Questions

🤔
Functional properties enabling large stepsize
What is the precise functional property of the loss that permits large-stepsize convergence? Self-boundedness ($\|\nabla^2 L\| \leq L$) works for logistic regression — what is the general condition?
🤔
Trackable trajectory measures
Sharpness and local mean are candidates, but neither fully captures the trajectory. What observable measure of the GD path predicts convergence in the unstable regime?
🤔
Early-phase feature learning vs NTK
Large-stepsize training escapes the Neural Tangent Kernel regime and learns features. A rigorous theory of this early-phase feature learning, especially its dependence on stepsize, is missing.
🤔
Large stepsize for SGD and Adam
The tutorial's results are for full-batch GD. The analogous theory for mini-batch SGD and for Adam (which has an unclear continuous limit even in the small-stepsize regime) is largely open.
💡
Better stepsize schedulers
Warmup, cosine decay, and the silver schedule all work empirically. A principled theory of optimal stepsize scheduling — especially how warmup navigates to flatter regions — is missing.
💡
Better optimizers
Preconditioning and normalization layers change the loss landscape in ways that interact with stepsize. Understanding this joint optimization of architecture and optimizer is an open design problem.
💡
Stepsize vs structure interactions
Attention mechanisms and network depth create structured loss landscapes. How do large stepsizes interact with these structures — particularly multi-head attention and residual connections?
💡
Other instabilities
Data quality, numerical precision (FP8/BF16), and gradient norm spikes in LLM pretraining are all forms of instability distinct from pure stepsize-driven oscillation. A unified theory is missing.
