Perioperative and long-term risks following nephrectomy in older live kidney donors

Muzaale, Abimereki D. — Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD, United States

NIH / NIA · K08 · 1K08AG065520-01 Project Start: 2020-04-01 Project End: 2024-12-31 Dept: Surgery · Schools of Medicine

The number of older adults (aged ≥55) undergoing live donor nephrectomy has quadrupled over the last two decades and this trend is likely to continue. However, a knowledge gap exists in our understanding of the resiliencies to physical stress in older donors. Beyond age-related vulnerability to surgical stress, this aging population may have significant glomerular senescence, an inadequate adaptive response to the 50% loss of nephron mass and, as such, a very high likelihood of CKD in their lifetime. Approximately 80% of older donors have a postdonation GFR<60 ml/min/1.73m² and meet the standard definition of CKD. As older donors progress towards CKD, they are likely to become frail and, as such, at increased risk of hospitalization. No study has focused on the identification of clinical signatures of resiliency in older persons undergoing live donor nephrectomy. The renal system is directly impacted by nephrectomy and its resiliency may affect the health and well-being of older donors. Provider discussions with these potential donors should focus on the patient's most important question: "Can I safely donate my kidney?" not the population-level question asked by the 2017 KDIGO guidelines: "How many ESRD events in younger donors may be averted by transplanting older donors instead?"

We propose the following scientific aims: 1. To quantify donation-attributable risk of ESRD and mortality in older donors; 2. To describe the prevalence of hospitalizations in older donors vs. older nondonors; and 3. To implement a risk calculator for ESRD, mortality and sentinel hospitalization in older donors.

Our efforts will culminate in the creation of online risk calculators that inform the conversation between candidates and providers about the propriety of donation. Dr. Muzaale will learn how sentinel hospitalization events in an aging cohort of older donors leave a footprint of present and missing data that will be methodically crucial in preparing an R01 proposal to characterize trajectories of resiliency from laboratory, physical, cognitive, and other tests.

Public Health Relevance

Over the last 20 years, the number of older adults (aged ≥55) undergoing live donor nephrectomy has increased 4-fold. In light of an aging US population, the new kidney transplant allocation system, and recent changes to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) practice guidelines, this trend is likely to continue. The goal of this project is to improve our understanding of the perioperative and long-term risks most pertinent to older donors in order to inform dialogue with care providers.

Digital twins (DTs) are virtual replicas of an individual's physiology, continuously updated with real-time multi-modal data (wearables, EHRs, sensors, labs, genomics) to simulate, predict, and optimize health trajectories. In the context of frailty resilience, they model vulnerability to stressors (surgery, hospitalization, illness) while quantifying recovery capacity—exactly the "resiliency to physical stress" gap your K08 grant highlights in older live kidney donors.

Frailty is dynamic: a multidimensional decline in reserves across physical, cognitive, and social domains. Resilience is the ability to bounce back (or not) after a perturbation. DTs turn this into actionable simulation: they treat sentinel hospitalizations (your "causal scars") as state-altering shocks that rewrite the trajectory, not mere noise.

Core Architecture for Frailty DTs

Most frameworks use layered designs. A 2025 scoping review on Hospital-at-Home (HaH) DTs for frailty proposes this practical 5-layer model:

  1. Sensing — Wearables (gait speed, grip strength, accelerometers), ambient sensors (motion, falls), physiological monitors, patient-reported outcomes.
  2. Communication — Bluetooth/Wi-Fi/cloud for real-time bidirectional flow.
  3. Storage — Secure cloud/EHR integration (handling sparse or missing data via harmonization).
  4. Analytics — Descriptive → predictive (ML, time-series, Markov chains) → prescriptive (what-if scenarios). Emerging use of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to embed biological laws for realistic frailty progression.
  5. Visualization — Dashboards, 3D models, alerts, or interpretable heatmaps (perfect fit for your "coloring book" concept).

The COMFORTage EU project (2025) adds an XAI layer for explainable recommendations and scenario simulation.

Real-World Applications in Frailty Resilience

  • Trajectory simulation & early warning: Predict transitions (robust → pre-frail → frail) or hospitalization risk using gait, nutrition, multimorbidity data. COMFORTage lists explicit use cases: gait sensors for fall risk, metabolic tracking for sarcopenia, cognitive-motor simulation for dementia/hospitalization overlap, frailty-informed triage in emergencies, and post-ICU recovery monitoring.
  • What-if resilience testing: Simulate interventions (exercise, deprescribing, nutrition) to forecast recovery speed after a stressor—precisely your "donation-attributable risk" and sentinel-event footprint.
  • Hospital-at-Home extension: Predictive analytics trigger alerts for deterioration; 2025 reviews map tools that could prevent readmissions in frail patients.
  • Broader aging DTs: AI-driven "Aging Digital Twins" integrate genetics + real-time metrics for personalized risk assessment and intervention in precision geriatrics.

Direct Tie to Older Kidney Donors & Your Grant

No published DT yet exists specifically for post-nephrectomy frailty in older donors (searches returned only general renal adaptation models and one liver-transplant DT). But the fit is seamless:

Here's a pseudocode sketch for a simple yet powerful regime-switching model tailored to your post-nephrectomy frailty/resilience trajectories in older donors. We'll use a Markov-switching / Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-style approach where regimes represent frailty states (e.g., robust, pre-frail, frail), and sentinel events (hospitalizations) act as explicit operators that force or bias regime transitions (your "causal scar" insight).

This is discrete-time, easy to implement in Python (e.g., via hmmlearn + custom extensions, or from scratch with numpy/scipy), and extensible to continuous observations (e.g., eGFR, frailty score, grip strength proxies).

Key Assumptions / Setup

  • Time steps: t = 1, ..., T (e.g., months or quarters post-donation)
  • Hidden regimes (states): K = 3 (robust=1, pre-frail=2, frail=3)
  • Observations: y_t = vector of markers (eGFR, frailty index, etc.) — can be multivariate Gaussian per regime
  • Transition matrix: Γ (K×K), where Γᵢⱼ = P(regime j at t | regime i at t-1)
  • Emission: y_t ~ Normal(μ_{s_t}, Σ_{s_t}) where s_t = current regime
  • Sentinel events: e_t = 1 if hospitalization at t, else 0
  • When e_t = 1 → override/normalize transition probabilities toward worse regimes

Pseudocode: Generative Model (Simulation / Forward Pass)

# Parameters (to be estimated later)
K = 3                  # number of regimes: 1=robust, 2=pre-frail, 3=frail
initial_prob = [0.6, 0.3, 0.1]   # π: starting regime probs (vector of length K)

# Base transition matrix Γ (time-homogeneous)
transition_matrix = np.array([
    [0.80, 0.18, 0.02],   # from robust
    [0.30, 0.50, 0.20],   # from pre-frail
    [0.05, 0.25, 0.70]    # from frail
])

# Regime-specific emission params (mean vector per regime, 1D for simplicity: eGFR proxy)
mu = [75.0, 55.0, 35.0]          # higher = better
sigma = [8.0, 10.0, 12.0]        # variance increases with frailty

# Sentinel shock parameters
shock_boost_to_worse = 0.4       # add mass to worse states when event occurs

# Simulate one trajectory
def simulate_trajectory(T, initial_state=None):
    states = np.zeros(T, dtype=int)      # hidden regime sequence s_1..s_T
    observations = np.zeros(T)           # y_t (e.g., eGFR-like)
    events = np.random.binomial(0.05, size=T)  # sparse sentinel events (~5% prob)

    # Initialize first state
    if initial_state is None:
        states[0] = np.random.choice(range(K), p=initial_prob)
    else:
        states[0] = initial_state

    observations[0] = np.random.normal(mu[states[0]], sigma[states[0]])

    for t in range(1, T):
        # Base transition probs from current state
        probs = transition_matrix[states[t-1]].copy()

        # Apply sentinel shock if event occurred at t
        if events[t] == 1:
            extra = np.zeros(K)
            extra[-1] += shock_boost_to_worse           # big push to frail
            extra[-2] += shock_boost_to_worse * 0.5     # some to pre-frail
            probs += extra
            probs = np.clip(probs, 0, None)
            probs /= probs.sum()                        # renormalize

        # Sample next state
        states[t] = np.random.choice(range(K), p=probs)

        # Emit observation
        observations[t] = np.random.normal(mu[states[t]], sigma[states[t]])

    return states, observations, events

def em_train(y, events, n_iter=50):

    transition = initialize_transition()
    mu, sigma = initialize_emissions(y)

        #        ξ_t(i,j) = P(s_t=i, s_{t+1}=j | y, events)
        gamma, xi = forward_backward(y, events, transition, mu, sigma)

        # M-step: update params
        for i in range(K):
            for j in range(K):
                num = sum(xi[t,i,j] for t in range(T-1))
                den = sum(gamma[t,i] for t in range(T-1))
                transition[i,j] = num / den

        # Emissions: weighted means/vars using gamma
        for k in range(K):
            w = gamma[:,k]
            mu[k] = np.average(y, weights=w)
            sigma[k] = np.sqrt(np.average((y - mu[k])**2, weights=w))

    return transition, mu, sigma

Extensions for Your Use Case

  • Multivariate emissions: replace Normal with MultivariateNormal (numpy.linalg)
  • Physics-informed bias: instead of fixed shock_boost, learn a logistic function of time-since-donation or baseline GFR
  • Random effects: add donor-specific offsets to mu (hierarchical Bayes / mixed HMM)
  • Continuous time: use CT-HMM if follow-up irregular (more complex)
  • Output to heatmap: at each t, posterior P(s_t | data) → color gradient (robust=green → frail=red), animate over time
