The naïve estimate is valid as a thought experiment: 45 years of continuous 8K visual input plus multi-directional audio plus per-second full-network weight snapshots yields something in the low hundreds of zettabytes. The arithmetic is defensible. The model is wrong.

The brain is not a surveillance archive. It is a predictive compression engine — closer to the Buganda agricultural calendar than to a hard drive. It stores only the high-error keyframes: the Budo Sunday roll call, the 2:02 AM message from Basaza, the Kitante reunion after 34 years. Everything else is reconstructed on demand from learned generative rules.

The Salk Institute's synaptic strength measurements give the human brain a total structural capacity of roughly 10–100 TB when counting effective bits per synapse at 4.1–4.7 bits rather than the 16-bit float assumption. But even that is the hardware envelope — the lived file is far smaller, because it is a model, not data.
